Where does the ray hit?

A pixel tells us which way to look. A plane tells us where to stop. Find the hit by measuring how quickly the ray closes the gap to the plane.

One example: The camera is at O. X points right and the optical axis Z points forward (up on the page). Each spatial diagram shows the Y = 0 slice, with coordinates in the order (X, Z).

The fitted plane is the wall X = 1 m. A vertical line represents its trace in this slice. Diagram 06 puts the measurements on a number line instead.

01 A pixel picks a ray

The ray starts at the camera and passes through the pixel's location on a virtual image plane. A pixel alone does not tell you how far along the ray to go.

Here: the pixel is at (0.5, 1) m on the image slice.

02 Give the ray one metre of forward depth

Move 1 m forward along Z and 0.5 m right along X. The combined move follows the ray. Its length is about 1.12 m, not 1 m.

q = (0.5, 1) is the direction per metre of forward depth. Its Z component is 1, but q is not a unit vector.

03 Pin down the plane

Pick any point on the plane, such as P₀. It need not be the point the ray hits. The unit normal n has length 1 and points perpendicular to the plane.

P₀ and P₁ are on the same plane: both have X = 1 m.

04 Read the straight-across gap

Draw an arrow from the camera centre O to P₀. Now keep only the part that goes across the wall, at a right angle to it. In this picture, P₀ = (1, 3) m: 1 m right and 3 m forward. The unit normal n = (1, 0) points right, so the 3 m forward does not count. The projected arrow ends at F = (1, 0) m, the closest point on the wall.

n · P₀ is the signed component of that arrow (P₀'s position vector) along the unit normal. The arrow is measured in camera coordinates, and its component has the same length units (metres). Positive means along n; negative means opposite n. Drop the sign: |n · P₀| is the perpendicular, shortest distance from the camera to the plane.

n · P₀ = 1 × (1 m) + 0 × (3 m) = +1 m.The wall is |n · P₀| = 1 m away. That's not the straight-line distance to P₀ (‖P₀‖ = √10 m) or the distance along the ray.

05 Read how much the ray closes the gap

Keep only the part of one q-step that goes in the normal direction. The step gains 0.5 m along +n for each metre of forward depth.

n · q = 1 × 0.5 + 0 × 1 = +0.5 metres of normal movement per metre of forward depth (m/m).

06 Divide gap by progress

At the hit, the ray has gained exactly the normal offset of the wall. Divide that signed offset by the signed gain per metre of forward depth.

Signed gap: n · P₀ = +1 mRate of gain: n · q = +0.5 m/mForward depth: t = (n · P₀) / (n · q) = (+1 m) / (+0.5 m/m) = 2 m

The arrows each show the gain from one metre forward. The result is a depth in metres, not a count. It can be fractional.

07 Put the hit back on the ray

Multiply both components of q by the depth. The result is 1 m right and 2 m forward, so the point is on both the ray and the wall.

P = t q = (2 m)(0.5, 1) = (1, 2) m.

08 Flip the normal, keep the same hit

With the normal pointing left, the wall offset is negative. The ray also moves opposite that normal. Both signs change and cancel in the division.

t = (−1 m) / (−0.5 m/m) = 2 m with −n, the same depth as with n.

09 Watch near-parallel rays

The parallel ray makes no progress toward this wall, so it never hits it. The near-parallel ray makes little progress, so a small direction error can change the hit depth a lot.

Parallel: n · q = 0; no hit on this wall.Near parallel: t = (1 m) / (0.1 m/m) = 10 m.

Nearly parallel intersections can be unstable. If the line intersection gives t < 0, it is behind the camera and not on the forward ray.

10 Tell the three distances apart

The gap gets you across to the plane. Z-depth gets you as far forward as the hit. Ray distance goes directly from the camera to the hit.

Range = ‖P‖ = √5 m ≈ 2.24 m. The right triangle has legs of 1 m and 2 m.
